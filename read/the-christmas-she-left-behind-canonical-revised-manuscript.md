# Chapter 1

[T]{.dropcap}he drive up from the airport had taken the better part of two hours. The interstate gave way to the narrower county roads that wound past dairy farms and covered bridges and the same handful of towns whose names she still knew by heart, though she hadn’t lived among them in seventeen years. Somewhere in the second hour Clara had turned the radio off. She didn’t want even a stranger’s voice in the quiet she needed for whatever this visit was about to be.

Clara had been sitting in the driveway with the engine off for four minutes, long enough that the kitchen window had gone bright, dim, bright again as her mother crossed in front of it, checking without checking. Clara had grown up in this house and still hadn’t decided whether that talent of her mother’s was a comfort or a surveillance system.

She got out before the porch light could do it a third time, leaving her phone face-up on the passenger seat where she could see it light up without checking it, a manuscript still open in her email from a meeting she’d left early to make her flight, a debut novelist’s third revision sitting half-annotated in a document she’d promised herself she wouldn’t think about again until Monday. She’d made herself that promise at the gate in Chicago. She’d already broken it twice, once over the beverage cart and once somewhere over Ohio.

The cold had a smell to it here that Chicago’s cold didn’t bother with, woodsmoke and wet leaves and something mineral underneath that she’d have called snow if there’d been any yet. There wasn’t. The lawn was that flattened, colorless brown that came before winter committed to itself, and the Fenwick sign at the end of the road still said TREES, though nobody would need one for another two weeks.

The sign snagged something loose in her before she could stop it. Anna, eleven years old, standing on the hood of their mother’s car in the Fenwick lot because she’d declared the sign needed correcting, dragging a fat marker across the bottom in block letters that took Hal a full season to sand off: AND MISFITS. She’d gotten in trouble for it, real trouble, the kind that came with a phone call to their mother at work, and had considered the whole episode a triumph anyway, recounting it at Christmas dinners for years afterward with the unrepentant pride of someone who’d never once regretted a single consequence in her life. Clara smiled before she’d registered why, standing in her parents’ driveway with her bag still in the car. A beat later, the smile cost her more than she’d expected it to.

The front door opened before she reached it.

“You made good time,” Margaret said, which was what she always said, regardless of the time made. She had flour on one forearm and an oven mitt tucked under the other arm like a clutch bag, and she looked Clara over quickly, checking for weight lost or color drained or anything else a mother cataloged and never mentioned. “Your father’s got the leaf out for the table. He’s decided this is the year we need it.”

“Do we need it?”

“No,” Margaret said. “But he needed to decide something, so.”

The entryway still held the same coat hooks, the same runner rug gone thin in the middle, the same brass dish by the door where car keys had accumulated for as long as Clara could remember. She set her bag down at the foot of the stairs without looking at where she was putting it. Her body knew this house better than she wanted it to. The house smelled like a Thanksgiving that had been building since roughly seven that morning, and under it, fainter, the smell the house always had that Clara couldn’t name and had stopped trying to. Thomas was in the dining room with the table extension leaned against the wall and a screwdriver he clearly didn’t need, and he looked up at her like he’d known she was there before she came in and had been waiting for a reasonable moment to acknowledge it.

“Clara.”

“Hi, Dad.”

That was the whole exchange. It always was. He came around the table and hugged her, one arm, a hand flat between her shoulder blades for exactly as long as a hug was supposed to last and not a second longer, and then he went back to the leaf, because affection in this family had a runtime.

She carried her bag up to her old room, which her mother had stopped calling her old room around the time Clara turned thirty and started calling the guest room, though nothing in it had changed. Same bedspread. Same water stain on the ceiling shaped like a state neither of them had ever agreed on. Clara set her phone face-down on the dresser, then picked it up again to check it, because she was already braced for an email that hadn’t come yet, and put it back down, annoyed at herself for both things.

Dinner ran competent and a little too quiet in the gaps, three people arranging themselves around a table built for more, the good china out for an occasion that was really just one of the four or five times a year anyone bothered to use it. Margaret talked. She always talked at dinner, an even, generous stream about the neighbors, about the church roof fund, about a woman from her book club, Carol Ann, who’d gotten engaged at sixty-one to a man she’d met, unbelievably, at a tire shop, both of them there for the same flat, and how the whole club had spent forty minutes on the memoir they were supposed to be discussing and the rest of the two hours on whether a tire shop counted as a meet-cute or just a coincidence with good timing. Clara let it move past her like weather, answering when a question surfaced, laughing when a laugh was clearly wanted, working through a plate of turkey that was, as always, slightly dry in the breast and entirely redeemed by her mother’s gravy, a fact the family had agreed upon without discussion sometime around 1998 and never revisited.

Thomas ate. Occasionally he said something short and dry enough that Clara had to replay it to be sure it had been a joke, and once, when Margaret described the tire-shop fiancé’s proposal in more detail than the story strictly required, down to the exact make of the tire, he said, without looking up from his plate, “I proposed to your mother in a Sears,” and Margaret said, “It was the parking lot of a Sears,” and Thomas said, “It was in the Sears. I’m not doing this again with the geography,” and Clara laughed before she could decide whether she wanted to, a real one, surprised out of her, and for a second the table felt like a table that had always had three people at it instead of four.

It was Margaret who brought her up first, and safely.

“Do you remember the year she did the place cards?”

“God,” Thomas said, into his plate.

“She was nine. She made place cards for Thanksgiving, which nobody asked for, and she did them in some sort of code so that you had to work out which seat was yours.” Margaret was already laughing at it. “She wouldn’t give anyone the key. Your grandmother sat in the wrong chair for the entire meal because she refused to participate on principle.”

“She sat in mine,” Thomas said.

“She sat in yours, and you let her, and you ate your dinner standing at the counter like a man in a bus station rather than make a thing of it.”

“I was comfortable.”

“You were not comfortable, Thomas.”

Clara laughed properly at that, and Margaret was pleased with herself, and for a while it was easy, because that Anna was nine years old and safely more than twenty years back and could be handled by all three of them without anyone getting hurt. They did another one after it, the summer she’d tried to keep a frog in the downstairs bathroom, and another, the retainer she had lost twice and then, the third time, admitted to having thrown into a lake.

Then Margaret started a sentence, “Well, you remember how Anna used to,” and didn’t finish it, because there wasn’t a version of finishing it that didn’t require everyone to sit inside the sentence for a while, and instead she got up to get the gravy that didn’t need getting. Thomas’s eyes went to the empty fourth side of the table and then back to his plate, quick, practiced, a reflex worn smooth from three years of use. Clara had gotten good at this particular kind of silence herself. She’d had practice in Chicago, at dinners with colleagues who knew and dinners with colleagues who didn’t, and she could feel the shape of the missing sentence the way you could feel a step that wasn’t there on a familiar staircase.

The version this family had, the one none of them had ever sat down and agreed to, was that Anna had left it too late. She’d been tired all that last autumn and had called it the diner. She’d gone in when she couldn’t call it the diner anymore, and there had been eleven days, and at the end of them a phrase Margaret had said once on the phone in January and never again in Clara’s hearing.

What nobody in this house said out loud was the part underneath it, which was that a person did not arrive at eleven days without having known something for a good while first, and having gone on not telling anybody about it.

Clara had been up for two days that Christmas. In on the twenty-fourth and out on the twenty-sixth, because a manuscript had gone into copyedit badly and she had told herself, at the time, that this was a reason. Anna had been quiet at the table. Clara had noticed it and had put it down to the diner, and to whatever was going on that year between Anna and their mother, and had gotten on a plane.

Then Margaret set her fork down with the particular deliberateness of someone about to say something she’d rehearsed. “I talked to a realtor,” she said. “Sandy Okafor’s cousin, does the whole county now. She’s coming by in the spring to look at the place.”

Thomas didn’t stop eating, which told Clara he’d known already.

“The house?” Clara said, unnecessarily.

“It’s too much house for two people,” Margaret said, gentle and final, the tone she used for facts she’d already made peace with and expected other people to catch up to. “I’m not asking anyone’s permission. I just wanted you to hear it from me instead of finding a sign in the yard next time you’re up.”

“Okay,” Clara said, because there wasn’t anything else honest to say yet. This visit already felt different from every other one she’d made in the last three years. Finite in a way it hadn’t been an hour ago.

They didn’t talk about it further. That, too, was a family skill, arguably the family’s best one: the ability to set something enormous on the table, let it sit there in plain view, and go on eating.

After dinner Thomas retreated to the garage to do something to the truck that didn’t need doing, and Margaret recruited Clara to help get the porch decorations down from the attic before the ladder got icy, which was less an attic errand than an announcement that Christmas was now officially permitted to begin existing in this house, a full month early.

The attic smelled like cardboard and cedar and, faintly, mothballs from a coat nobody had worn since the Clinton administration. Margaret went after the porch garland, methodical, sneezing twice, narrating the whole time about which box was which even though none of the boxes were labeled in any way that matched what she said. Clara worked her way along the far wall, where the ornament boxes were stacked three high, soft cardboard gone furred at the corners.

She pulled the top box down to check it wasn’t the porch stuff and the flap gave more than she expected, spilling two ornaments loose across her palm. A glass bell, cracked along one side and kept anyway, the way this family kept everything cracked. And under it, small enough that she almost missed it in the dim attic light, a plain round glass ball, the kind that came a dozen to a box from the drugstore and never got used because they were too ordinary next to the good ones.

Except this one had been opened. Someone had pried the little metal cap loose and pushed something inside before pressing it back down, not quite flush, so that when Clara turned the ornament over, something inside shifted and clicked against the glass.

Clara sat back on her heels with the ornament in her palm for a second before she did anything else, turning it once so the seam in the metal cap caught the bare attic bulb. It was heavier than an empty ornament had any business being. Somebody had wanted this found eventually, not immediately.

She worked the cap free with her thumbnail. A key fell into her palm, small, brass, the kind that belonged to nothing she could immediately picture, an old-fashioned skeleton shape you didn’t see much anymore, the teeth worn soft at the edges like it had been used a long time before it had been hidden. Folded twice around it, so small she nearly missed it too, a strip of paper gone slightly furred at the folds.

She knew the handwriting before she’d finished unfolding it. She’d have known it anywhere, on a grocery list, on the back of a photograph, on anything. Small, slanted hard to the right, the *y*’s looped like they were in a hurry to get somewhere else.

*For when you’re ready. A.*

Clara stayed kneeling on the plywood floor longer than the sentence should have taken to read. Somewhere below her Margaret was still talking, something about the garland’s lights being half burned out last year, and the ordinary sound of her mother’s voice kept coming up through the floorboards while Clara sat there with three years of managed distance sitting uselessly in her hands.

“Find the garland?” Margaret called from the other side of the attic, muffled by a stack of boxes.

Clara closed her hand around the key until the teeth of it pressed a line into her palm. “Not yet,” she said, and her voice came out steadier than she’d have guessed it could. “Still looking.”

She put the ornament in her coat pocket, key and note and all, and did not put it back in the box.

The house went quiet by ten, Thomas’s footsteps on the stairs, the particular creak of the third-from-top step that nobody had ever fixed because everybody had stopped noticing it decades ago, the furnace ticking on and off somewhere below. Clara lay on top of the guest-room bedspread in her clothes and turned the key over in the lamplight until she’d memorized the exact worn place on its teeth, the exact weight of it, without getting any closer to knowing what it opened.

She tried the obvious things first, because trying the obvious things was easier than sitting still with the note. The dresser drawers, all four, though none of them had ever locked in her lifetime. The lockbox of her own, back in Chicago, that held her passport and a savings bond from her grandmother, though that key lived on her own ring and always had. She held it up to the lamp again and read the note a fourth time, a fifth, as if a sixth reading might change what it said, or when it had been written, or why it had taken this long to find her.

*For when you’re ready.*

Ready for what. She had been home four hours.

At some point past midnight she gave up pretending she was going to fall asleep and lay there instead listening to the furnace and turning the small brass shape over and over between her fingers, worn teeth, worn teeth, worn teeth, until the repetition stopped meaning anything at all and became a thing her hands were doing while the rest of her stayed wide awake in the dark.

# Chapter 2

[P]{.dropcap}riya called on Friday morning while Clara was walking the back property with a mug of coffee she didn’t want, mostly to get a signal strong enough to hold a call and mostly, if she was honest, to get some air that didn’t have two other people’s breathing in it.

“I’m not going to pretend this isn’t terrible timing,” Priya said, which was how Priya opened most conversations that were about to be good news wrapped around something that would cost Clara sleep. “The board approved the imprint. They want an editorial director in place by spring, and they want it in Chicago, in the building, not the two-days-a-month arrangement you’ve got now. I fought for you to be the first call. I’m making it.”

Clara stopped walking. Ahead of her, past the sagging half of the split-rail fence nobody had gotten around to fixing since she was in high school, the old equipment shed still had a row of Fenwick’s paper tree tags nailed along its back wall, going back God knew how many years, faded orange and curled at the corners, one for each season the girls had come home with a tree from the farm up the county road. She hadn’t looked at that wall in years. She was looking at it now.

“That’s an incredible offer,” Clara said, and meant it, and also felt a real vertigo at meaning something completely while another part of her was doing arithmetic it hadn’t asked to do.

“It’s a real one. Relocation package, real title, real budget, your name on the list going forward instead of somebody else’s initials in the corner of it.” Priya’s voice had gone warm, and she actually cared about the answer, not just the org chart. “I know you’ve got the hybrid thing going with the Kowalczyk list and it’s working, and I know I’m the one who set that up for you in the first place. This isn’t that. Ownership wants a director who’s actually in the building three, four days a week, at least for the first year while the imprint finds its feet. I can’t spin that into something it isn’t.”

“No, I get it,” Clara said, and did. “When do they want an answer?”

“Officially, end of January. Unofficially, the longer you sit on it, the more it looks like hesitation to people who don’t know you, and I’d rather that not happen to you.” A pause, papers shifting somewhere on Priya’s end, the sound of an office Clara could picture down to the light fixtures. “You don’t have to say anything today. I just didn’t want you finding out from an all-staff email over turkey.”

“I’m literally at my parents’ for Thanksgiving right now.”

“Then I definitely didn’t want you finding out over turkey.” Priya’s laugh was quick and real. “Go be with your family, Bennett. This’ll keep for a few weeks. It’s not going anywhere and neither, I hope, are you.”

Clara thanked her, said the right things, hung up, and stood there long enough that the coffee went from too hot to not worth finishing. Behind her the house sat squat and lit and thoroughly itself against the gray morning, the same house that, come spring, would have a stranger walking through it with a clipboard, measuring it for a life without her family in it. In front of her, an old shed wall kept a record of every Christmas tree two sisters had ever dragged home, color-coded and curling.

It took her a moment to place why the offer had landed harder than a piece of good news should. Then she had it. This was the same shape as the offer she’d turned down at twenty-eight, almost exactly, a title and a relocation and a version of her career finally catching up to the work she’d already been doing, except that time she hadn’t turned it down so much as let it slip away, quietly, one missed deadline and one skipped interview at a time, while she learned how to manage her grandmother Eleanor’s medications and translate the specialist’s language back into something the rest of the family could carry. Nobody had asked her to give up the promotion. She had simply run out of hours in which to hold both things, and the promotion, being patient and inanimate, had waited exactly as long as it needed to before going to someone else. Seven years later, here it was again, dressed differently, offered by a different mouth.

She tried, briefly and unsuccessfully, to picture the actual shape of saying yes. An apartment in a different neighborhood than the one she had now, closer to the office, the kind with a doorman who’d learn her name, which her current one hadn’t in six years. A calendar that filled itself in without her input. A version of her life that looked, from the outside, like the thing she’d been working toward since she was twenty-four years old, and that was, she suspected, precisely the problem with trying to picture it standing in her mother’s backyard the day after Thanksgiving. From here it didn’t look like a life. It looked like a very well-organized escape route.

She walked closer to the shed than she meant to, close enough to read the tags, most of them just a year in Anna’s quick slanted print or her own tighter one, a couple further back in their mother’s hand from before either of them had been old enough to hold a marker steady, and at the far end of the top row a few older than that, the ink gone brown, in a square hand she didn’t know. 2011 was there, and 2014, and a run of three consecutive years where the tag just said MISFIT in block capitals with no date at all, which had been the whole point back then, that the date didn’t matter, only that they’d gone. She didn’t remember who’d started calling them that. She remembered that it had been the two of them, every year, arguing good-naturedly over which lopsided, overpriced, unsellable tree deserved to come home with them instead of a proper one, and that their mother had eventually stopped fighting it and just bought a second, better tree for the front window so the misfit one could live wherever the girls decided it belonged that year.

She remembered one year specifically, standing right about where she was standing now, thirteen years old maybe, arguing with a nine-year-old Anna over a tree so crooked it had practically been growing sideways, Anna dead certain it deserved to win because, in her exact words, delivered with the moral authority only a nine-year-old could summon, *it’s trying its best and nobody’s going to give it credit for that if we don’t.* They’d bought it. Their mother had rolled her eyes the whole drive home and then spent forty-five minutes helping them wire it upright against the living room wall so it wouldn’t tip over, because apparently a tree trying its best deserved that too. Clara hadn’t thought about that sentence in twenty years and had it word for word.

She hadn’t thought about the tags in years. She hadn’t had a reason to stand this close to that wall since the last year they’d gone together, which she could date exactly and had been declining to, and going alone had never once occurred to her as a thing a person could do.

She didn’t tell anyone at breakfast. It wasn’t a secret so much as it was a thing that needed somewhere to live in her head first, and her head, this particular week, was already fully booked.

That evening, over leftovers reheated into something that tasted better than the original meal had, Margaret brought the house up again, more carefully this time.

“I know I sprang that on you yesterday,” she said. “I’ve been going back and forth on how to say it for months, and then it just came out sideways at the turkey, which is exactly how your grandmother used to break news, so I suppose it’s genetic.” She refilled Clara’s water without being asked, an old habit. “I don’t want you rushing off after two days like you usually do. I know your work doesn’t stop just because it’s a holiday. But this might be the last one in this house, and I’d like you here for the whole thing, if you can manage it.”

“I can manage it,” Clara said, and was a little surprised to hear herself say it before she’d actually decided.

“You don’t have to say it just to make me feel better,” Margaret said, watching her a little too closely for someone who’d supposedly already accepted the answer.

“I’m not. I’ve got the vacation days. I haven’t used any of them in two years, which my HR department has opinions about.” That much, at least, was true, and saying it out loud made the rest of it easier to mean. “I can work from here if I need to. Nobody at the office cares where the emails come from as long as they come.”

“Well,” Margaret said, and did not look up from the water glass she was refilling, which was how Clara knew she had wanted it badly. “I’ll take it.”

Thomas, across the table, said nothing, but he got up a moment later and came back from the kitchen with a plate that had clearly been hidden behind the orange juice, a last unclaimed wedge of pie he’d apparently been rationing to himself in installments since Thursday, and set it down in front of Clara without comment, which was as close as her father ever came to saying *good, stay*.

“Is that the pie you told Mom was gone?” Clara said.

“I told her the pie *plate* was empty,” Thomas said. “Which it was, once I’d finished with it the first time.”

Margaret swatted him with a dish towel that had clearly performed this exact function many times before, and for a moment the kitchen held something that felt, almost, like the ordinary shape of a family instead of the managed one Clara had been navigating since Thursday.

They lingered at the table longer than usual, the dishes going cold and unwashed for once, Margaret telling a long, meandering story about a dispute at church over whose casserole recipe had been unfairly excluded from the holiday potluck rotation, Thomas interjecting periodic dry commentary that made the story funnier than it had any right to be. Clara laughed more in that single hour than she had in months back in Chicago, where laughter tended to arrive in the efficient, professional register of colleagues being pleasant to each other rather than this looser, sillier version, the kind that came from people who’d known each other’s worst jokes for thirty years and still, somehow, found them funny.

“You should stay up a little,” Margaret said, when they’d finally cleared the table, Clara drying while her mother washed, an old division of labor neither of them had to discuss. “There’s a whole box of old Christmas movies in the den, the actual tapes, if you can believe your father still has a machine that plays them.”

“I threw out the machine twice,” Thomas called from the other room. “She keeps buying a new one.”

“I keep buying a new one because Christmas isn’t Christmas without the version where the dog ruins the tree,” Margaret called back, unbothered, and Clara smiled at the domestic rhythm of it, an argument they’d clearly had a hundred times before, neither of them actually invested in winning it.

She didn’t stay up for the movie, not that first night, too tired from the flight and the day’s accumulated weight to do anything but climb the stairs and fall into a bed that still, somehow, remembered the exact shape of her seventeen-year-old self.

The key was still in her coat pocket, and the coat was on the back of the door, and the furnace moved the air in that room enough that every so often, faintly, she could hear it.

# Chapter 3

[S]{.dropcap}he walked past the mudroom twice on Sunday before she let herself stop there.

The first time was on her way to get the recycling bin, and she noticed that the panel of tongue-and-groove paneling under the coat pegs sat very slightly proud of the wall around it, a hairline of shadow along one edge where the others were flush. She kept walking. There was a version of the rest of her morning where she didn’t clock it, where a slightly misaligned piece of wood paneling in a house full of slightly misaligned things stayed what it looked like, and for about four minutes she tried to live in that version.

The second time was on her way back, and she stood in front of it for a full ten seconds with an empty recycling bin in her hands, doing the math on how strange it would look if her mother walked in and found her staring at a wall. Margaret was two rooms away, running water for the good pot, singing along under her breath to something on the radio that Clara couldn’t place, unaware that her older daughter was standing in the mudroom conducting a forensic examination of the coat pegs.

Nobody walked in. She set the bin down.

The panel came away easier than she expected, no screws, just pressure-fit into a shallow frame someone had built for this purpose a long time ago, and behind it was a cavity barely bigger than a breadbox, empty of everything except a metal lockbox with a small brass keyhole set into its face, painted the same flat gray as everything else the Bennetts had ever owned that was meant to survive a basement flood.

Clara already had the key in her hand. She’d been carrying it in her coat pocket since Thursday, which she told herself was practical and knew, even as she thought it, was superstition.

It turned on the first try.

Inside, seven pieces of paper, folded in thirds, numbered in the corner in pencil, and beneath them, flat against the bottom of the box, a red envelope, sealed, with two words on the front in block capitals so careful they’d clearly taken more time than anything else in the box: LAST. CHRISTMAS EVE.

Clara sat down on the mudroom floor with her back against the cold radiator and read all seven, out of order at first and then again in the right one, because she’d never been able to leave a numbered thing unsorted in her life.

*One. Fenwick’s. Worst tree on the lot. You know the rules, you’ve just been pretending not to for three years.*

*Two. Find Louise Bell. Ask her what I did for her. She’s not going to want to tell you. Make her.*

*Three. Attic, the blue trunk under the eaves, shoebox marked WINTER. There’s a photo on top. Give it to Dad. Don’t explain it. He’ll know.*

*Four. The panel in the mudroom, under the coat pegs. There’s more behind it than this box. Don’t read what’s inside until you’re actually ready. I mean that one more than the others.*

*Five. Historical Society, the Christmas Eve Auction, whenever they’ve actually scheduled it this year, you know how Louise is about the calendar. There’s something of ours there. Go get it back.*

*Six. Whatever Daniel tells you, forgive him before you open the last one. I need you to do this one out of order if you have to. Trust me on this even though I know exactly how that’s going to land.*

*Seven. Don’t open until Christmas Eve. I know you, Clara. I mean it. Don’t.*

She read the first one twice more. It was almost funny. Then it wasn’t almost. A real laugh startled halfway out of her before it caught on the rest of what she was holding. *Worst tree on the lot. You know the rules, you’ve just been pretending not to for three years.* That was Anna exactly, bossy and correct and unbothered by the fact that she was dead, still finding a way to be right about something Clara had absolutely been pretending not to know.

The second one she read twice before the name registered properly. Louise Bell. It meant nothing to her, a stranger’s name in her sister’s handwriting, and the not-knowing sat strangely alongside how certain the instruction sounded, like Anna had assumed Clara would know exactly who this was and had been wrong about that, or hadn’t cared, or had wanted Clara to have to go find out. *She’s not going to want to tell you. Make her.* Clara had no idea what a person could possibly have done that someone else wouldn’t want to admit to. She filed it away with everything else she was going to have to go collect in person.

The third stopped her. A photograph for their father, something specific enough that Anna trusted him to recognize it without an explanation, which meant it was something the two of them shared that Clara had never been part of. She tried to picture what kind of photograph earned that kind of confidence and came up empty.

The fourth she read three times, because the instruction underneath the instruction, the *don’t read what’s inside until you’re actually ready,* felt less like caution and more like a warning her sister had considered carefully before writing it down. Anna had never once in her life told Clara to be careful about anything. Anna threw herself at things and let the landing sort itself out. And she had stopped, here, long enough to write this down.

She read the paragraph about managing her own legacy three separate times, and each time it landed a little differently, admiration curdling briefly into resentment and then into a third thing she didn’t have a word for. *You even managed how I’d remember your dying,* she thought, and let the thought sit there, unsoftened, refusing to forgive it just because the rest of the letter had earned so much good faith already.

She saw, too, what the letter left out. Anna had not only put the call off until tomorrow. She had asked Margaret to wait, and she had picked up the phone once with Clara’s number on the screen and let Daniel talk her out of it, and none of that was here. She had taken the whole of it onto herself in two sentences, *that’s on me, not on you,* so that nobody else would have to carry any of it. It was the last thing she managed. Clara let her have that one.

Clara read it again, start to finish this time, and somewhere in it something broke open, quiet at first and then not quiet at all.

She cried the way she had not at the funeral, where she had organized a reception and answered condolence cards and been the capable one, because somebody had to be and it had not occurred to her that there would be a bill. She cried for the argument and the ten days and the eleven weeks. She cried for the misfit trees and the wrapped oranges and Louise’s roof.

She cried hardest at the paragraph about the legacy, which was the only part of the letter that had not tried to be kind to itself, and which was therefore the only one she believed completely.

She sat with the silence, letting it be what it was rather than reaching for something to fill it. “I don’t forgive you for all of it,” she said, quieter, testing the sentence the way you tested a bruise. “Not yet. Maybe not ever, completely. But I understand you tried to love me the only way you knew how to, even when the way was wrong, and I think that’s going to have to be enough for both of us, because it’s the only version of this either of us actually gets.”

Nobody answered, because nobody could, and Clara sat with that fact, permanent and unarguable, and let herself cry through the whole weight of it without once trying to manage the crying into something smaller or more convenient.

Eventually, when the worst of it had passed, she got up, unsteady, and walked the downstairs rooms slowly, the letter still in her hand, touching things without quite meaning to, the doorframe where their childhood heights were still marked in faded pencil, the kitchen table where Anna had once declared, at eleven, that she would never eat another Brussels sprout as long as she lived and had, infuriatingly, kept that promise for the rest of her life. Clara stood in the mudroom a long moment, in front of the panel, flush and ordinary now, and thought about the whole strange architecture of this month, everything Anna had built into a house Clara had once been in such a hurry to leave. Then she went back to the tree, sat back down in the same spot, and let herself be found.

Margaret found her first, coming through from the kitchen, and didn’t ask what the letter said. She sat down on the floor beside her, careful of her good dress, and put an arm around Clara’s shoulders, and Thomas followed a moment later, lowering himself down on Clara’s other side with considerably more effort than either of the women, and neither of them said a single word. They sat there and let Clara’s grief take up as much space as it needed.

At some point, Margaret reached over and took the letter gently from Clara’s lap, not to read it, simply to hold it steady while Clara wiped her face, a small, practical kindness offered without comment, and Thomas rested his hand briefly between her shoulder blades, the same economical gesture he’d offered in the hall two weeks ago. Nobody asked Clara to explain herself. Nobody suggested she’d cried long enough. The three of them sat there, under the tree, in the quiet, for as long as it took.

It was Thomas who noticed the shape at the window first, a truck pulling into the driveway, headlights cutting briefly across the dark yard before going dark themselves, and Clara, following his gaze, saw Daniel through the glass, a wrapped gift in hand, clearly meant for Thomas, hesitating at the edge of the porch light when he caught sight of the scene inside, visibly reconsidering, already half-turning back toward his truck.

Clara was up and at the door before she’d fully decided to move.

“Don’t go,” she said, opening it before he’d finished turning away, her face still wet, her voice still unsteady, not caring how unguarded she looked doing it. “Please. Come in.”

Daniel looked at her for a long moment, snow starting to fall again behind him, fine and steady, and his face did the thing it did when he had decided something and hadn’t said it yet.

“Okay,” he said, quiet, and came inside, out of the snow, into the tree light.

He didn’t ask what the letter said, didn’t press for details she wasn’t ready to give, simply set the wrapped gift down for Thomas and settled onto the floor beside her, close enough that his shoulder found hers, offering nothing but his presence. Margaret brought out a fourth mug of cocoa without being asked, an easy, wordless welcome, and Thomas, on the other side of Clara, reached over and clasped Daniel’s shoulder once.

They sat together a long while, the five of them, four living and one remembered, the tree glowing steadily above them, the star catching the light the way it must have caught it decades ago, on a night none of them had been alive to witness. Nobody explained anything further. Nobody tried to. Margaret hummed a few bars of something off-key and didn’t finish it. Thomas fell asleep sitting up for a few minutes and woke himself with a snore loud enough that even Clara laughed, wet-faced, unable to help it. It wasn’t resolved. It was simply, for the length of one evening, enough.

# Chapter 39

[C]{.dropcap}hristmas morning arrived bright and full, the particular fullness of a house with everyone in it who mattered, Margaret up before anyone else fussing over a breakfast nobody needed her to fuss over, Thomas contentedly wrong about at least one assembly instruction on whatever gift required assembling, Daniel there at the table without anyone treating it as remarkable, simply another chair filled, another voice added to the easy, overlapping conversation of a family that still argued about the coffee and still occasionally went quiet in the exact same places it always had, imperfect in all its familiar ways, and entirely hers.

There was a moment, early, before the gift-opening properly started, when Clara caught sight of the whole scene at once, her father laughing at something Daniel had said, her mother refilling coffee nobody had asked her to refill, snow falling steadily outside the window.

Gifts were exchanged with the usual chaos of a family that had never been especially organized about the process, Thomas’s attempt at assembling a bird feeder for Margaret devolving into a comic saga that had everyone laughing by the time he got it standing upright. Ruthie arrived mid-morning uninvited and entirely expected, with cinnamon rolls and a joke mug for everyone, WORLD’S OKAYEST printed above a blank space each recipient was meant to fill in. Thomas declared his read WORLD’S OKAYEST MECHANIC on the spot, in permanent marker, to general applause.

Daniel had made her a small wooden box, the kind of thing he built between larger jobs, and when she opened it and found it empty he said, quiet, “For whatever you decide is worth keeping.” She’d had to look away for a moment.

The day itself was long and slow, nobody with anywhere to be. There was a walk after lunch that Margaret proposed and Thomas objected to on principle and then led, out along the back property and down the fence line to the equipment shed, where Clara showed Daniel the wall of Fenwick’s tags, orange and curled, one for every tree two sisters ever dragged home and a few at the far end nobody had explained, and Daniel read them all the way to the end without saying anything and then said, “Somebody should take a photograph of this before spring,” which was, Clara thought, the single most useful thing anybody had said to her all month.

Ruthie stayed for the afternoon and fell asleep in the good chair by three, which Margaret declared a Christmas tradition on the spot, over Ruthie’s sleeping objections. Thomas got the fire going and then spent forty minutes adjusting it, on the theory that a fire left alone would go out, a theory nobody in the family had ever managed to disprove to his satisfaction. At some point somebody put on the radio and there was, inevitably, a saxophone rendition of a carol, and Clara said, “Oh, that’s Anna’s,” out loud, without bracing for it first, and Margaret said, “God, that record,” and Thomas said he had thrown it out twice, and nobody’s face did the thing it used to do.

Later, in the quiet after the plates, Margaret asked Clara to help her carry a box up from the basement and then didn’t, in the end, ask her to carry anything, and the two of them stood down there among the shelves of things that would have to be decided about before spring, and Margaret said, “I’m going to need help with this in March,” and Clara said, “I know,” and that was the entire conversation.

She and Daniel found a quiet moment on the back porch late in the afternoon, coffee in hand, the cold sharp and clean around them, and stood together without needing to fill the silence with anything at all, his hand finding hers, easy now.

“Strange, isn’t it,” Daniel said eventually, looking out over the snow-covered yard. “A month ago I’d have told you there wasn’t a version of this Christmas where I got to stand here with you like this.”

“A month ago I’d have told you I wasn’t staying past New Year’s.”

“Funny how that works out.” He glanced at her. “I keep thinking about the first day I saw you again. You were at your mother’s kitchen window and I was getting the ladder off the truck, before either of us had said a word. I remember thinking, there she is, and being terrified of exactly how much that thought still meant to me after all those years.”

“You never said anything.”

“Didn’t seem like my place to, given everything I was already carrying.” He squeezed her hand. “Glad I get to say it now, though. There she is. Still true. Truer, actually, now that you know the worst of me and decided to stay anyway.”

“I didn’t decide easily.”

“I know. That’s what makes it mean something.”

“I told Priya yes,” Clara said, eventually, breaking the silence not because it needed breaking but because she wanted to say it out loud to him specifically. “Hybrid. Two weeks a month in Chicago, the rest here. I’m not choosing between them. I’m keeping both.”

Daniel was quiet a moment longer than the answer needed. “I’d rather have you here all of it,” he said. “I’m not going to pretend otherwise, and I’d be lying if I said two weeks a month is what I’d have picked. But I’m telling you that instead of doing anything with it, because it’s mine to feel and it isn’t mine to decide, and I’ve had a fair amount of practice lately in the difference.”

“You could have just said good.”

“I could have. That’s the version where you find out in three years what I actually thought.” He looked out at the yard. “Not doing that one again.”

“I know,” Clara said. “That’s part of why I could ask for it in the first place.”

He smiled at that, and didn’t say anything else, and they stood together a while longer, watching snow settle over a yard that had held, this month, more of Clara’s life than she’d let it hold in three years.

One year later, Christmas Eve again, the Bennett family home sold in April, a quiet, practical transaction Margaret had handled with more grace than Clara would have managed in her place. Clara and Daniel had a place of their own now, a small house on the edge of town he’d restored himself over the course of the year, slowly, the way he did everything. The imprint had launched in the spring, hybrid, as promised. Five of the six were under contract. The Wren estate was still deciding, as it had been deciding since March of the year before, and the review had been moved to February, and Clara had stopped pretending to herself that she wasn’t counting the weeks.

The chain ran on its own now, out of Ruthie’s counter and Hal’s card table, and still with no names on anything. Clara and Daniel had spent the fall making it something other people could join without anybody having to be thanked. This year’s list had nine houses on it. Jess had crossed off two.

Sal had come to dinner twice that fall and had taken to Thomas immediately, the two of them arguing about baseball from before either Clara or Daniel was born. She still went to him on Tuesdays, mostly, and brought the good crossword and heard the road map story twice more and did not stop him either time.

The photograph hung in the back hall by the door, where you saw it going out rather than coming in. Daniel had taken it in March, on a gray Saturday with the sale two weeks off and the ground still hard, forty exposures of the same wall until he had one where the light came in low enough to read every tag. Clara had stood behind him for most of it being told that this was in fact a difficult photograph and being asked, twice, not to help.

There were fifty-one tags in it. More years than the two of them had been going: the oldest were nailed up by a man who took his own boy out to Fenwick’s one Saturday every December until he didn’t. Thomas had stood in front of the print in April for a long time and then put one finger on the far end of the top row and said, “That’s his hand,” and gone out to the car. She had counted them in the spring and had not counted them since, and she had stopped, at some point in the autumn, seeing the wall at all and started seeing the hallway, and had not expected to mind quite so little.

The shed had come down in June. Somebody had wanted the lot cleared.

Clara stood in front of their own tree that evening, Daniel beside her, and reached into the box of ornaments for the one she’d carried with her the whole year, the plain glass ball, the key sitting inside it again where she’d put it back the night she finished the list, and had left it since.

“Every year,” Daniel said, watching her hang it.

“Every year,” Clara agreed.

There was still a word she hadn’t said to him. It had been most of a year and it hadn’t come, and she had stopped waiting for the morning it would, and he had never once asked her for it. Some evenings she thought it was close. Tonight she didn’t, particularly, and he stood beside her anyway with his hand at the small of her back, and neither of them brought it up, because they had both gotten a great deal better at not deciding things on each other’s behalf.

She stepped back to look at it, the glass catching the tree lights the way it had caught the bare attic bulb that very first Thanksgiving night.

She’d smiled, hanging it, without effort, without performance, without the careful management she’d spent three years applying to every feeling she’d ever had about her sister.

Margaret and Thomas arrived a little later, driving over from the smaller place they’d settled into that spring, and the four of them spent the evening imperfectly, warmly, Thomas once again wrong about some assembly instruction, Ruthie stopping by briefly with a pie nobody had asked for and everybody was glad to have.

Margaret caught Clara alone in the kitchen at one point, both of them refilling mugs of cider, and studied her with the look she got when she had something honest she wanted to say.

“I told your father the rest of it, finally. About the call. What she asked me to do, and when. In the spring, before we sold the house.” Margaret said it, no longer needing to brace for the confession the way she once had. “He wasn’t angry, not the way I expected. Just sad, mostly, that I’d carried it alone for so long. We talk more now. Not always well. Real things.”

“I think she’d be glad, actually. Not smug about it. Glad.”

“I think you’re right.” Margaret picked up both mugs, and stopped in the doorway. “We talk more now. I said that already. I want to be honest that it’s not always an improvement.”

“No?”

“Some of it I’d have rather kept.” She said it without any self-pity in it whatsoever. “I’m doing it anyway.”

She went back out to the living room.

*Go live something enormous, Clara. I mean it.*

She had. She was. Daniel’s hand found hers again, and together they watched the ornament catch the light.
