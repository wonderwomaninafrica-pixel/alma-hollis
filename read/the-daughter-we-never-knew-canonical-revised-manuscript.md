# Chapter 1

[T]{.dropcap}he furnace had been off for three weeks, and the house had taken the cold into its walls the way it took everything, slowly and without complaint.

Nora felt it the moment she let herself in, that particular interior cold, worse somehow than the weather outside because it had settled and made itself at home, the cold of a place that had stopped being lived in and had not yet been told. She kept her coat on. She stood in the front hall a while with her mother’s keys still in her hand, four of them on a ring shaped like a small brass acorn, the edges worn smooth by forty years of a thumb, and she did not go any farther in, because going farther in meant beginning, and she was very good at beginning things and had discovered, in the last three weeks, that she did not know how to begin this one.

From the kitchen came the small conscientious sounds of a man she had met eleven minutes ago opening and closing the cabinets one at a time. He was thorough. She would give him that.

“These the original windows?” he called.

“I have no idea,” Nora said, which was true, and then, because it sounded unhelpful, “Probably. She wasn’t one for replacing things that still worked.”

That part was also true, and it landed somewhere under her ribs before she could stop it, so she made herself move, went and stood in the kitchen doorway to make the landing stop. The inspector, whose name was Ray and whose clipboard held a form with more boxes on it than she would have believed a house could generate, was crouched in front of the sink with a small flashlight, looking up into the dark under the counter as if it might tell him something her mother never had.

“Little bit of staining on the trap,” he said. “Old. Not active. I’ll note it.” He noted it. He noted everything, in a small square hand, and he had a way of saying I’ll note it that made each thing sound both forgiven and permanent, the way a doctor says we’ll keep an eye on that. He straightened, knees popping, and ran a thumb along the edge of the counter where the laminate had lifted a little from forty years of dishwater. “They don’t build the cabinets like this anymore,” he said, not unkindly, to nobody. “Solid. You could stand on these.” He opened the one under the window, the one where the good glasses lived, and shone his light into the back of it, and Nora had to look away, because there was something about a stranger’s flashlight going into the backs of her mother’s cabinets that she had not been prepared for and did not have a place to put.

“How old is she, the house,” Ray asked.

“Nineteen sixty-something. My parents bought it before I was born. My father did the back porch himself, in the eighties. My mother says it’s not to code.” She heard the tense come out wrong, says, again, the grammar of it not yet updated to match the facts.

“Nothing from the eighties is to code,” Ray said comfortably, and made a note.

He worked through the downstairs unhurried, opening things, and Nora trailed him at the distance you keep from a person doing a job you can’t help with, answering when he asked and otherwise learning her own house through his eyes, a strange and unwelcome education. The scuff by the back door where forty years of shoes had come off. The window in the dining room that had never shut right and that her mother had wedged with a folded index card the way she wedged everything, temporarily, permanently. The radiator that ticked. A stranger was taking the measure of all of it, cataloging the ordinary wear of the only place Nora had ever been a child, and putting a small dollar sign of consequence beside each thing, and there was nothing wrong with any of it, it was his job, it was the whole point, and it made her feel obscurely like she was watching someone go through her mother’s purse.

Denise was in the dining room being cheerful at nobody. Nora could hear her out there, moving the good chairs a few inches so the light would come through better for the photographs she had already taken twice, narrating her own competence in a bright low voice. Denise had sold four houses on this street. She said so the way other people say grace, and she had said it in the car that morning, and it had been a mistake, because now Nora could not unhear it.

“The buyers are just going to love the light in here,” Denise had told her, on the drive over, and that had been a mistake too. The buyers. A young couple from outside Boston, a teacher and someone who did something with hospitals, moving out to the Berkshires for the reasons people moved out to the Berkshires, more room and less money and the belief that a slower life was a thing you could simply decide to have. They had a baby coming in the spring. Denise had mentioned it as a selling point, and Nora supposed that from Denise’s side of the table it was one.

They would love the light. Nora stood in the doorway and looked at the long dining table where the light was, allegedly, so good, and thought that they would put a different table under it. They would not know that the burn mark near the head of it, sanded and stained over so it was only a shadow now, a darkness in the grain you had to know to find, was from a Thanksgiving when Nora was nine and had set a hot trivet down a half second too late, and that her mother had not been angry, had only turned the platter so the scorch faced the wall and said we’ll call it character, and had meant it, and every Thanksgiving after had seated Nora at that end so the shadow faced her like a small private joke between the two of them. The buyers would not know that. They would not know anything, and that was the whole grim mechanism of selling a house. You handed a stranger a set of rooms and you kept the rooms that were actually yours somewhere behind your eyes, where they cost you rent for the rest of your life.

“Ma’am, you want the heat back on for the walkthrough, or you good?” Ray asked, from the hall.

“I’m good,” Nora said. “I’ll only be a couple of hours.”

He gave her a look that was not unkind, the one that started to say something and then thought better of it. People had been giving her that look for three weeks, at the funeral home and the bank and the church basement and now here, the look that got as far as I’m sorry for your and then reconsidered, and Nora had gotten efficient at heading it off. She smiled the particular way you smile to end a sentence, and Ray took it, and went to find his ladder.

The kitchen was the room the house had happened in. She knew that the way you know a thing without ever having said it. The living room had been for the television and for company that never came, and her mother’s bedroom was a place Nora kept with the door most of the way shut and passed quickly, the way you cross a cold floor. But the kitchen had been where Diane Vance conducted her life, at the small table under the window, with the calendar on the wall by the fridge, and the calendar was still turned to October, which was the last month her mother had believed in. Nora stood and looked at it. A church raffle, penciled in. A dentist. And the seventeenth circled twice, hard, in the blue pen her mother kept on a string taped to the side of the fridge so it wouldn’t wander off. Circled twice. Nora had noticed it the first day and had not been able to make herself find out what the seventeenth was, and she did not do it now either, because there was a whole category of small unanswerable things in this house lately and she had learned to walk around them the way Ray had walked around the soft board on the porch.

She opened the freezer instead, because it was something to do with her hands.

It was a disaster of grief, and she had forgotten. In the first days people had brought food the way water finds the lowest place, and her mother’s big chest freezer out in the mudroom was full, but so was this one, the small one over the fridge, packed past reason with foil packages in other women’s handwriting. Marion’s lasagna, three of them, each labeled MARION LASAGNA in fat black marker as though there were competing lasagnas, as though a lasagna could go astray and need to be returned to its owner. A soup from the Petersens with the date on the lid and, underneath the date, the single word SORRY, which Nora had decided to read as a description of the soup. A foil brick with no name on it at all, dense as a paving stone, that could have come from anyone and would, she suspected, outlive them all, the cockroach of casseroles. She stood there and let the cold breathe out at her face and did the math on it, which was easier than the other math. Eleven, twelve containers. She was one person, here for the weekend. Peter had offered to come and she had told him not to, and now the freezer looked back at her like a decision she was going to have to explain to somebody.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. Peter.

*how’s it going out there*

She looked at it a second. *Fine,* she typed. *Inspector’s here. Denise is out here rearranging my dead mother’s chairs.* Then she deleted the second sentence, because it was funny, and she did not want to be funny at him from two hours away, did not want to hand him the little joke and have him be relieved by it, have him think she was okay because she could still make the joke. She sent the one word alone.

*good,* he wrote back. *call me tonight. love you.*

She put the phone in her pocket without answering that last part, and shut the freezer, and the little magnet that had held nothing to the freezer door, a smiling ceramic cat that had been on some refrigerator or other Nora’s entire life, slid off with the motion and hit the floor and broke, cleanly, into three pieces.

Nora looked at it.

“Everything okay in there?” Denise sang.

“Fine,” Nora said. “Just a magnet.”

She crouched and picked up the pieces. The cat had broken along lines that were almost clean, the head, and then the body in two, and she found she was holding them in her palm and fitting them back together, head to body, the way you would fix a thing you meant to keep, and there was no glue in this house that she knew of and no earthly reason to mend a broken magnet for strangers with a baby coming, and she did it anyway, held the three pieces in register in her cold hand a moment as if pressure could do what pressure couldn’t. Then she wrapped them in a paper towel and put them in the pocket of her coat, next to the keys, and stood up, and her knees cracked, and she was forty years old and her mother was dead and there was a man in the next room noting things.

“Ray,” she called, and her voice came out level, which she was proud of, “how are we doing on time?”

“Making good progress,” Ray called back. “Roof’s your only real question mark. I’ll want to get up there before the light goes.”

The light. It was not even three and the light was already thinking about going. That was November in this town, the sun giving up around half past four as if it had a better offer somewhere, the hills going the color of a bruise and then the color of nothing, the valley folding itself away early. When she was a kid the early dark had felt like being tucked in by the whole county, the streetlights coming on down the hill, the smell of other people’s suppers. Now it felt like the day was being taken from her before she had finished with it.

Denise appeared in the doorway with her coat already on and her keys already out, wearing the particular energy of a woman who had somewhere to be at four and wanted you to know she would fit you in first. “I think we are in great shape,” she said. “Ray, you’ll email me the report? Nora, honestly, this is the easy part, I mean it. Some of these estate situations, oh, you would not believe. I had one, brother and sister hadn’t spoken in eleven years, everybody with a lawyer, we had to do the whole thing through email because they wouldn’t sit in a room. You are lucky it’s just you. Simple.” She said it warmly, a compliment, the way you would tell someone they had good bones. “One signature and you’re done.”

“Just me,” Nora agreed.

She walked Denise to the door and stood on the porch while the agent picked her way down the front steps in shoes that were wrong for the season, calling back a last cheerful thing that the wind took most of. The maple by the road was bare. It had been the last tree on the street to give up its leaves every autumn, a stubbornness her mother had remarked on every single year as if the tree were showing character, holding out, and it was bare now, and the leaves were down in the gutter along the road going soft and dark and returning to dirt. A neighbor Nora didn’t recognize lifted a hand from across the street, the vague salute of a person who knew who she was and what had happened, and Nora lifted hers back, and they were both spared having to say anything, which was, she was learning, the great mercy of a small town, that people would let you not say it.

The cold came up off the porch boards through the soles of her shoes. She thought, distantly, that she really should get the heat on, that she was being stubborn like the tree, that there was no medal for standing in a freezing house proving she could take it. She stayed where she was another minute anyway. Above her, on the roof, Ray’s ladder scraped and settled, and then his weight came onto the shingles over the porch, slow and careful, a stranger walking on the top of her childhood, noting things.

Inside, she made herself useful, which was the only religion she had ever really had. She found the marker and the roll of blue painter’s tape in the drawer where such things had always lived, the drawer being the one part of the house she could still predict, and she went room to room putting a small strip of tape on the underside of the furniture that was coming with her and leaving bare the furniture that was going to the estate sale, which was most of it. The good chairs, no. The dining table with its sanded shadow, no, though her hand hovered. Her mother’s reading lamp with the scorched shade, she put a strip of tape on that, and then peeled it off, and then put it back, and left it, and could not have told you why the lamp and not the table.

She had expected to be sad and was not, and this worried her the way a light left on in another room worries you, not urgently, just steadily, a thing not right. She kept waiting for it to arrive. She had done the funeral correctly, every part of it, and she had not cried, and she was not refusing to. There was simply a door somewhere in her chest that had not opened, and she had a house to sell and a life two hours east and a job that had granted her exactly the number of days the handbook allotted for a mother, and she did not have the luxury of standing around waiting for a door to do a thing doors did on their own schedule or not at all.

One signature and you’re done. That, at least, was clean, and she could hold on to clean.

She was in the hall closet, sorting her mother’s coats into a bag for the church and a bag for the trash and finding it strangely hard to tell which pile a coat belonged in until she was actually holding it, the weight of it, the smell of the collar, when her phone rang. Not Peter. A number she didn’t know, local exchange, the 413.

“This is Nora Vance,” she said, wedging the phone against her shoulder, a wool coat still over her arm, the good camel one her mother had worn to the things that mattered, to weddings and to funerals, to Nora’s own wedding.

“Ms. Vance, hi, this is Karen, I’m calling from Berkshire County Title, we’re handling the title work on the Elm Court property?” A pleasant, careful voice, the voice of a person about to make a small thing sound like nothing at all. “So sorry to catch you on a Saturday. I did leave you a message yesterday, I wasn’t sure it went through.”

“It might have,” Nora said. “It’s been a week. What can I do for you?”

“So, it’s probably nothing,” Karen said, and something in Nora’s whole body understood, in the half second before the woman went on, that it was not going to be nothing, because probably nothing was the thing people said at the top of the stairs right before they told you how far down it went. “We pulled the deed for the closing and we just need to confirm a little bit of information before we go any further. It’s fairly routine, honestly, we see it more than you’d think. But I do need to get it sorted out before we can clear title over to your buyers.”

The camel coat was heavier than it had any right to be. Nora shifted it to her other arm.

“Sort out what,” she said.

“Well,” Karen said, and there was the sound of paper, of a finger going down a line. “The deed of record isn’t in your mother’s name alone. It looks like she recorded what’s called a life estate deed a while back, which isn’t unusual, people do it to keep the house out of probate, so on paper the property passed automatically when she, ah, when she passed. That part’s actually clean.”

“Okay,” Nora said.

“It’s just that the ownership that came into effect, the remainder interest, it isn’t only yours.” A small pause, and Nora would remember the smallness of that pause for a long time afterward, the professional gentleness of it, a woman who had plainly done this before and still did not like it. “There are two names on it, Ms. Vance. Yours, and one other. And before we can sell the property, I’m afraid we’re going to need both of you to sign.”

Somewhere over her head Ray’s footsteps crossed the roof and stopped.

“That’s not right,” Nora said. Her voice was very reasonable. She was proud, even then, of how reasonable it was. “It’s just me. There isn’t anyone else. It’s a mistake, it’s a clerical thing, you’ve pulled the wrong lot number, or, the wrong Vance, it’s a common enough name.”

“I did wonder that,” Karen said kindly. “I checked it twice. It’s the right property, it’s the right parcel. The other name is on the recorded deed, in your mother’s own filing, from,” here the paper again, “from eight years ago.”

“Whose name,” Nora said.

And Karen told her, and Nora stood in the hall closet of the house she had grown up in, holding her dead mother’s best coat over her arm, in the failing blue light of a November afternoon, and heard the name of a woman she had never in her life heard of, spoken plainly down the phone as if it were a fact that had always been true and was only now, at a quarter past four on a Saturday, being read aloud to the one person it had been kept from.

# Chapter 2

[T]{.dropcap}he name was Frances Doyle, and Nora spent the first hour deciding it belonged to a stranger’s mistake.

This was a thing you could do, she found. You could take a fact and hold it out at arm’s length and turn it in the light and look for the seam, the place where it stopped being true, and if you were methodical about it you could keep it out there for quite a while, not touching you. So she sat down at the small table under the kitchen window in her coat, in the last of the flat afternoon light, with the cat magnet in three pieces in her pocket and the camel coat still over the back of the other chair where she had set it down, and she was reasonable at Karen for as long as Karen would let her be.

A clerical error, Nora said. A prior owner whose lien had never cleared. A woman with a name close to her mother’s, a Vance two towns over, a Prescott, a mix-up at the registry, they happened, everyone said so. Karen was patient in the way of a person who has watched a great many people arrive at the same wall from the same direction and knows there is nothing to be done but let them put their hands on it and feel that it is a wall.

“I do understand this is a surprise,” Karen said, twice, and the second time Nora heard how badly the word was doing its job, how far short of the thing it was falling, surprise, as if she had walked into a room and found people holding a cake.

She got off the phone. She stood in the cold kitchen a while and then she called Peter, because Peter was the person you called, and because some animal part of her wanted a voice that had known her longer than an hour. He answered on the second ring with the television going behind him, the ordinary Saturday of her actual life carrying on two hours east without her, and he said all the right things, which was the trouble. He said they would figure it out. He said it was probably a title glitch, those happened, his cousin had one on the lake house that took a month to untangle and turned out to be nothing. He said don’t spin yourself up before you know what it is, in the calm voice he used, the voice he had once used on their daughter, Laura, when she was small and now, increasingly, used on Nora, the low steady doctor-voice, and she stood there and found she was gripping the phone hard enough to feel her own pulse in the side of her hand.

“Peter,” she said. “It’s her handwriting on the calendar. She circled the seventeenth twice, in October, hard, and I don’t know what the seventeenth is. And there is a woman on the deed to this house, and I have never once in my life heard her name. So I don’t actually know what it is. That’s the whole thing I’m telling you. I don’t know what it is.”

There was a silence with weather in it.

“Come home,” he said gently. “Come home tonight, deal with it Monday with a lawyer. You don’t have to sit up there and figure it out alone in a cold house.”

“I can’t come home,” Nora said. “There’s a man on the roof.”

She had not meant it to be funny. It came out flat and true, there is a man on the roof, and Peter laughed, once, surprised out of himself, and she almost did too, and for a second the thing in her chest loosened its grip a quarter turn, and she was grateful to him in a way she could not have said, for laughing, for being a person who would laugh. Then Ray knocked politely on the frame of the back door to tell her the flashing around the chimney would want attention and that he would be off, and the moment closed over like water, and she told Peter she would call him that night, and did not entirely mean it.

Monday she sat across a desk from Ms. Ackerman, who had done her mother’s small flat-fee estate and who now had the decency to look genuinely sorry as she turned her monitor around so Nora could see.

The office was above a shoe store on North Street, one room and a reception nook and a radiator that ticked exactly like the one at home, and out the window the town went about its gray November business, and on the screen was a photograph of a piece of paper. That was all it was. A legal sentence sitting in the middle of a great deal of legal furniture, stamped and initialed, scanned a little crooked by a clerk years ago. Diane M. Vance, reserving unto herself a life estate, the remainder to her daughters, Nora J. Vance and Frances R. Doyle, as tenants in common, share and share alike.

Her daughters. The registry did not editorialize. It only wrote down what people came in and asked it to write down, and eight years ago her mother had driven somewhere Nora had never known her to go and asked a stranger to write down the word daughters, plural, in a document she had then never once mentioned, through eight years of Sunday phone calls and Christmases and the whole long back half of a life, right up until a month ago when she had stopped being able to mention anything at all.

“Share and share alike,” Nora read off the screen, as if reading it aloud might make it release its meaning, or give it up.

“It’s a common phrasing,” Ms. Ackerman said. “It means an equal, undivided half to each of you.”

“Half,” Nora said.

“I know.”

“She doesn’t get half of this house. She’s never set foot in it. I don’t even, I have never heard of her, do you understand, this isn’t an estranged aunt, this isn’t somebody I fell out with, this is a name, this is a name off a piece of paper.” Nora heard her own voice doing something and reeled it back in, and folded her hands on the edge of the desk. “How is this real. How did she do this without a single person on earth knowing.”

“She’d have needed to sit down with an attorney and sign it in front of a notary,” Ms. Ackerman said, carefully, because the sentence had a shape they could both see forming in the air between them. Her mother had gotten into the car on some ordinary Tuesday eight years ago and driven to an office and sat down across a desk exactly like this one and told a man what she wanted, and spelled out a stranger’s name, letter by letter, D-O-Y-L-E, and signed her own beneath it, and then had driven home and put something on for dinner and said nothing, and had gone on saying nothing for eight years.

“Can I break it,” Nora said. “Can I fight it. She wasn’t, there’s a thing, undue influence, or she wasn’t competent, she was old, somebody talked her into it.”

“Eight years ago your mother was fifty-five years old and a year a widow and running the parish rummage sale and doing her own taxes,” Ms. Ackerman said, not unkindly. “There’s no undue-influence case in a woman that sharp adding a beneficiary on her own initiative, quietly, with nobody in the room. I wouldn’t advise it, and I’d be taking your money to try, and I’d rather not do that to you on top of everything.”

So they went through the rest of it, and the rest of it was a wall with exactly one door in it, and Nora made herself look at the door.

To sell the house whole, both owners had to sign the deed at closing. Nora could, in theory, sell her own undivided half to somebody, but no living human being on earth wanted to buy a one-half interest in an empty house in Pittsfield and become a stranger’s forced co-owner on paper, and the buyers, the nice young couple with the baby coming in the spring, wanted the whole house, cleared and clean, and their patience was a resource with a bottom to it, and the bottom, according to the purchase agreement Denise had been so proud of, was a closing set for the sixteenth of December.

There was one other road. Ms. Ackerman said the word the way you would point across a room at something you would rather not touch.

“Partition,” she said. “You’d file a suit, ask the court to order the property sold and the proceeds divided, over her objection if she’s got one. Judges do it. But understand what it is, Nora. It’s a lawsuit. It’s months, not weeks. It’s fees on both sides, and the fees come out of the house before either of you sees a dollar, and you’d blow past your buyers’ closing date somewhere around the first hearing. You’d be suing a woman you have never met to force the sale of the house you grew up in, and the whole valley would have your business by Ash Wednesday, and at the end of all of it a court would sell it anyway, most likely for less than it’s worth right now, to somebody who is not your nice young couple.” She let that settle. “It’s the nuclear option. It exists. I would very much rather it stayed on the shelf where it is.”

Nora looked at the photograph of the deed on the screen, the crooked scan, the stamp, share and share alike. She thought about her mother in a chair like this one spelling a stranger’s name out loud. D-O-Y-L-E. Five letters her mother had known well enough to spell.

“She knew her,” Nora said. It was not a question. “For her to put the name down like that, spelled right, she had to have known her name. This isn’t a woman who found out she had a claim and came looking. This is a woman my mother went and found and wrote down.”

Ms. Ackerman did not answer, which was, Nora understood, an answer.

That night she did not go back to the house. She could not have said, if pressed, why she could not sleep in the house, only that she could not, and so she took a room at the motel out by the highway, the one with the buzzing sign and the ice machine down the exterior walkway, and she sat on the polyester bedspread with her shoes still on and put the name into her phone. Frances Doyle. It did not take long. There was a small business, Doyle and Daughter, no website worth the name, a cleaning service out of a place called Miamisburg, Ohio, with four reviews, all of them kind, all of them mentioning a woman named Frankie, Frankie is a lifesaver, Frankie does the baseboards nobody does the baseboards, we’ve had Frankie for years and wouldn’t trust anyone else in the house. And there was a face, one grainy photo tucked behind a logo of a cartoon broom, a woman maybe Nora’s own age or a little older, dark hair going gray at the part, squinting a little at whoever held the phone, half a smile, the smile of a person who did not especially want her picture taken but was being a good sport about it.

Nora sat on the edge of the bed in the buzzing dark and looked at the small squinting stranger who owned half her mother’s house, and she felt the ground she had been standing on her entire life tip a few degrees and stay there, tilted, and nothing on it in quite the same place as before.

Doyle and Daughter. So the woman had a daughter. So there was, somewhere in Ohio, a person who was Nora’s mother’s granddaughter, whom Nora’s mother had also never once mentioned, who existed on the far side of a silence Nora had lived her whole life inside without ever knowing it was a room with walls. A granddaughter. A whole branch of a family, a whole set of people with a broom on a logo, and her mother had gone to every one of Nora’s swim meets and sat in the same seat and driven her home and said nothing.

She did not decide, that night, to be reasonable. She was too tired for reasonable, and too tilted. But she lay back on the bad bedspread with the sign buzzing pink through the curtain and she thought about the partition, about a courtroom and a judge and the valley knowing her business, and she thought about her mother driving somewhere alone to spell a stranger’s name, and she understood, dully, that before she went to war she was going to have to at least look the woman in the face and find out what her mother had done, and to whom, and why, if there was any why to be had, because there was a version of this in which the woman in the broom photograph was a grifter who had gotten to a lonely old woman, and there was a version in which she was something Nora had no word for yet, and Nora could not fight either one of them until she knew which she was looking at.

She called Ms. Ackerman in the morning, from the motel, before the coffee had even finished in the little machine, and told her to send the letter. Formal, correct, one lawyer to a stranger. There is a matter concerning the estate of Diane M. Vance that requires your attention.

She thought she was buying time. She thought she was being careful, doing it right, keeping the thing on the leash of process while she figured out her footing. She did not know she was pulling a pin. She would not know it until the woman was sitting at her mother’s kitchen table squaring a folder with two fingers, and by then it would be far too late to have done anything else.

# Chapter 26

[T]{.dropcap}he winter taught them how to be sisters over the phone, which turned out to be its own skill, and they were not, at first, any good at it.

They had learned each other inside a pressure cooker, a house and a deadline and a dead mother, all of it intense and close and constant, and now they had to figure out how to have a relationship the ordinary way, at a distance, in the gaps of two busy lives, and the ordinary way was harder in its own quiet fashion. Frankie was bad at the phone. She’d spent her life keeping people at exactly the distance from which she could not be hurt, and a sister you called on Tuesdays was a person you were choosing to let close on purpose, over and over, with no house forcing it, and some Tuesdays Frankie let it ring out and texted later, sorry, slammed, and Nora learned not to read too much into it, and Frankie learned to call back.

The rubber tree became the whole comedy of that winter, and it started as a joke and turned, as things did with the two of them, into something that wasn’t quite a joke anymore.

Nora had taken the rubber tree home rather than let it die, and had texted Frankie a photo of it installed in her Boston kitchen: custody arrangement, I get her weekdays. Frankie had sent back a photo of Buster looking unimpressed. The dog demands visitation.

“She dropped a leaf,” Nora reported one February night, mock-grave. “I think it’s my fault. I moved her.”

“Of course you moved her, you can’t leave anything where it is.” Frankie’s voice down the line, warm and ordinary. “They drop leaves, it’s what they do. My God, you’re going to be a nightmare about the actual house, aren’t you, you’re going to repaint the whole thing by June.” And Nora stood in her Boston kitchen next to their mother’s plant, laughing, having someone to call about a dropped leaf.

They had their first ordinary fight in February, and it was, Nora noted afterward with a kind of wonder, a completely normal fight, an ordinary sister fight about an ordinary thing, no dead mother in it at all. It was about the house. Nora, being Nora, had run the numbers and thought they should rent it, a nice long-term tenant, it would cover the taxes and the upkeep and even throw off a little, it was the sensible thing, the thing that made the headache pay for itself. And Frankie had gone quiet on the phone in a way Nora was learning to recognize, and then said, flatly, “So strangers. You want strangers living in it.”

“Paying strangers. It’s an asset, Frankie, it’s just sitting there costing us money.”

“It’s not an asset, it’s the house, it’s the only, it’s where she.” Frankie had stopped. “I don’t want strangers in it. I know that’s not sensible. I don’t care. I spent my whole life being sensible about that house from six hundred miles away, being reasonable, being no trouble, and I’m done being sensible about it. I want it to be ours. Empty and costing us money and ours. If that’s stupid then it’s stupid.”

And Nora, the old Nora, would have marshaled the numbers, would have gotten two steps ahead, would have won. But she was not quite the old Nora anymore, and she sat with it, and she heard the thing underneath it, which was a woman who had been handed a family too late and was not about to rent it out to strangers, and she said, “Okay. No strangers. We keep it for us. You’re right, it’s not an asset, I was doing the thing, I was managing it instead of feeling it, you can tell me when I do that.” And Frankie had been quiet, and then said, “I’ll for sure tell you when you do that,” and it had been fine, better than fine, it had been the first fight they ever had and both walked away from, no door softly closing, no wet shoes, just two sisters disagreeing about a house and then not disagreeing about it, and hanging up still sisters. They’d had a fight and nobody left.

What the fight had not been about, and what neither of them said out loud that February, was that Nora could afford the house and Frankie could not.

Frankie did the arithmetic at the end of February, on the back of a client invoice, where she did all her arithmetic. The Hendersons did not come back in February as she had promised Dana they would. The Pruitts had gone to a girl out in Centerville who charged eleven dollars less an hour and did not do the baseboards, and the Beavercreek account had never happened at all. Call it fourteen thousand dollars of a year, gone out the back of one winter. Against that, her half of a house in Pittsfield that would have wired into her account the week before Christmas and had not, because she had signed the other paper instead. Against that, the taxes, which came to about forty-five hundred a year split two ways. Against that, a roof the inspector had called the only real question mark back in November, sitting up there over an empty house all winter with nobody underneath it to care.

She had never in her life been able to want a thing she could not pay for. That was the whole architecture, the nine-minute bag and the business you could run out of a car: you kept your wants down to the size of your means so that nobody ever had to be the one who covered you. And here she was at forty-four, in the black by nothing, holding half a building six hundred miles away that she could not live in and could not sell, and the strange part, the part she turned over with the radio off on the drive out to a job in Kettering, was that it did not feel like the floor going. It felt like a bill she had picked out herself. There was a difference, and she had needed forty-four years to find it, having spent all of them paying off bills that other people had run up in her name.

She told Nora about the fourteen thousand in March, on a Sunday, because that was apparently a thing you told a sister. Nora went quiet in the way Frankie was learning meant she was doing sums, and then said, carefully, that she could carry the taxes a few years and Frankie could pay her back or not. And Frankie said absolutely not. And Nora said okay. And then Nora said, all right, we split them, and you tell me if a spring comes when you can’t, and you actually tell me, you don’t just quietly pay it and eat beans. And Frankie said yeah. Okay. And meant it, and got off the phone and sat in the van in a parking lot in Ohio a minute with both hands on the wheel, because she had just handed somebody the whole arithmetic of herself, the bottom of it, the real number, and nothing had come back in an empty envelope.

By March they had a rhythm. Sunday calls, mostly, the old day, Nora’s mother’s day, repurposed now, and it made Nora smile every time, that she still made a Sunday call, that she’d handed the ritual down to something new. They talked about nothing, mostly, the good nothing, Wes and Laura and the plumber and the weather in two states and the endless deranged saga of the rubber tree, which was apparently thriving to a degree Frankie found personally suspicious. They were becoming, slowly, over a whole winter of Tuesdays and Sundays, the thing they’d been robbed of, the ordinary thing, the sisters who called each other about nothing.

And underneath all the ordinary, the date sat waiting, the fourth of April, in ink, on both their calendars, the first time they’d see each other since the gas station, and neither of them said how much they were counting on it, because they were their mother’s daughters and the counting-on was the hardest thing to say, but they both crossed the days off, six hundred miles apart, waiting for spring and the house and each other.

They opened the house on the fourth of April, the two of them, exactly as written.

Frankie drove out from Ohio and Nora up from Boston and they arrived within an hour of each other on a raw bright spring day with the snow finally gone off everything and the stubborn maple just thinking about its buds, and they stood in the driveway a minute grinning at each other like fools, because it had been almost four months of phone calls and texts and photographs of a rubber tree with increasingly deranged captions, and here they were in the flesh, in the driveway of the house they’d refused to sell, the two of them, sisters, on purpose.

The house had wintered all right. A pipe had wanted looking at and Frankie knew a guy now, because Frankie always knew a guy within a week of arriving anywhere, and they spent the morning on the practical things, the co-owners of a house doing the co-owner things, arguing amiably about what two people do with a house they only visit, landing on for now let’s just keep it and see, which was, they both knew, not a plan, but they were getting comfortable with not-a-plan, the two of them, they were learning that you did not have to have the whole field mapped to walk out into it.

Marion came over on the second morning, Easter, straight from Mass with a coffee cake, because Marion had a radar for an occupied house, and she stood in the bare kitchen looking at the pair of them with the frank interested eyes of a woman who has been waiting five months for somebody to explain something and has been about ten percent too polite to ask.

“Now,” she said. “You didn’t sell it.”

“We didn’t sell it,” Nora said.

“I know you didn’t sell it, Denise told half the county in January, I’m asking why.” Marion set the coffee cake down on a counter that had nothing else on it at all. “And I’ll say one thing and then I’ll mind my business.
