A Novel by Alma Hollis

The Boy Who Owed Us Nothing

Deb Hale has ninety-one days before the boy she raised ages out of foster care, and the law stops calling them a family. A quiet, deeply felt novel about the love the paperwork never recognized.

Chapter One

The steam table at Deerbrook Falls Middle School held four hundred and twelve trays of chicken and rice by six forty-five in the morning, and Deb Hale had learned a long time ago that you could tell everything about a day by how the rice looked at six forty-five. Today it looked fine. A little dry at the edges of the pan, nothing a splash of broth wouldn’t fix. She fixed it.

“You’re doing the thing,” Marisol said, from the sink.

“I’m not doing a thing.”

“You’re doing the thing where you fix something that wasn’t broken because you don’t want to think about the thing that is.”

Deb set the ladle down harder than she meant to. Behind her the steam rose off the rice in a soft, unbothered column, like it had nowhere else to be. Outside the high square windows the parking lot was the color of a nickel, salt-stained and half-frozen, and the sky over Route 4 hadn’t fully committed to morning yet.

“April third,” Marisol said, when Deb didn’t answer. “You’ve had it circled since before Christmas.”

“I don’t have it circled.”

“You have it circled in red pen with a box around it, Deb. I’ve seen your calendar. You keep it on the fridge.”

She did keep it on the fridge. She’d looked at that box more times than she’d admit to, the way you touch a bruise to see if it still hurts, except this bruise hadn’t happened yet. Ninety-one days out. She hadn’t done the math on purpose. It had just arrived one morning while she was making coffee, the number sitting there fully formed, the way numbers did to her sometimes, and she hadn’t been able to get rid of it since.

“He’s got school today,” she said instead. “He’s got the Bio test. And a shift at four.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know what you asked.”

Marisol let it go, because that was the deal between them, eleven years of working a lunch line together teaching a person exactly how far to push and when to hand somebody a rag instead. Deb took the rag. Wiped down a counter that also didn’t need it. The cafeteria smelled like it always did this early, bleach and browning butter and the particular metal smell of four hundred trays waiting to be filled, and there was something almost holy about it, doing the same motions in the same order at the same hour, five days a week, for going on twenty years. Nobody in this building needed her to be anything other than reliable. She had gotten very good at reliable.

The rice went out at eleven-fifteen and was gone by twelve-fifty and the pan came back with about a cup left in it, which was the correct amount and which Deb noted with the flat satisfaction of a woman whose ordering had been right.

She had a system for the rice and had had it since 2011. You made eleven percent more than the count suggested, because the count was based on trays and not on sixth graders, and sixth graders ate rice the way water finds a drain.

Bev said, at the pass, “You did eleven percent again.”

“I always do eleven percent.”

“One of these days you’ll do ten and we’ll all find out what happens.”

“We know what happens,” Deb said. “It happened in 2014.”

“It was not that bad in 2014.”

“There were four hundred children and we ran out at twelve-forty and I have never fully recovered.”

* * *

At home, three miles east, Eli Vance was standing in front of the bathroom mirror doing the thing he did before school, which was nothing dramatic, just a slow, careful check, shirt collar, hair, the small scar above his eyebrow that had healed pale and thin and that he touched sometimes without noticing he was doing it. He counted his shirt buttons under his breath, out of some old habit that had started at the parts counter, where you counted the same box of spark plugs three times before you trusted the number, and he did the same thing now with buttons, with steps to the bus stop, with the exact number of days he had left in this house, though he tried not to let that last one turn into an actual count. It turned into one anyway. Sixteen weeks. Give or take.

He didn’t tell Deb he counted things. It wasn’t a secret so much as it was nobody’s business, the way his mother’s name wasn’t anybody’s business, the way the group home before this one wasn’t, the way a lot of things about the first fourteen years of his life had quietly filed themselves under nobody’s business and stayed there. Deb had never asked him to unfile any of it. That was one of maybe six things about her he’d trust with money, if it came to that.

He grabbed his bag. Down the stairs two at a time, the fourth one creaking the way it always did, and into the kitchen where Deb had left a plate covered in foil like she did most mornings even though she was already gone to the school by the time he was up, egg and toast, the toast slightly overdone the way he liked it and she pretended not to remember was a preference.

There was a Post-it on the foil. Bio test. You got this. Fridge has extra OJ. D

He ate standing up, the way he ate most things, and did not let himself think about the box on the calendar, the one he’d also seen, the one he assumed she thought he hadn’t.

* * *

The kitchen at Deerbrook Falls Middle School had been built in 1968 and remodeled once, badly, in 1994, and Deb Hale knew every failure in it the way you know the sounds your own house makes at night.

The number two oven ran forty degrees hot and had since 2016 and the district’s position was that it was within tolerance. The floor by the dish pit had a low spot that held water, which was a slip hazard she had reported eleven times in writing. The walk-in’s compressor was original. There was a light over the prep table that had never worked in twenty years and that she had personally given up on in 2011.

And it all worked. That was the thing she was proud of and had never once said out loud to anybody, including Marisol: that four hundred and twelve children ate at eleven-fifteen every single school day out of a room with a hot oven and a wet floor, and had done for two decades, and the reason it worked was that a woman had built a system on top of the failures instead of waiting for the failures to be fixed.

You put the rolls in number two and pulled them four minutes early. You kept the yellow cone by the dish pit permanently, not as a warning but as furniture. You did the walk-in inventory yourself on Fridays because the compressor would go one day and when it went you would need to know exactly what was in there within ninety seconds.

That was the job. Not the cooking. The cooking was the least of it.

Marisol came past with a hotel pan and said, “Two’s hot.”

“Two’s always hot.”

“I know two’s always hot. I’m telling you the rolls are in two.”

“Then pull them at nine minutes.”

“That’s what I’m doing. I’m narrating.” Marisol slid the pan onto the rack. “I like to say it out loud so that when the district finally replaces that oven there’ll be a record that we’ve been compensating for eight years.”

“There’s a record. It’s eleven emails.”

“Twelve,” said Marisol. “I sent one in November you don’t know about.”

* * *

The bus was late, which put him at the school with four minutes instead of ten, which he minded more than he’d have admitted to anyone. Eli liked margins. He liked knowing where the edge of a thing was before he got to it. The Bio test he wasn’t worried about; Ms. Alvarez ran a fair room and he’d actually studied, actually understood the Punnett squares this time instead of just pattern-matching his way through them, and there was a small clean satisfaction in that, in a thing making sense because you’d made it make sense.

He got a ninety-four. He knew it before she handed it back, because he could feel when he’d gotten something right, a kind of quiet in his chest that didn’t need confirming, and getting the paper back with the number on it was less like finding something out and more like watching someone else agree with what he already knew.

At the parts counter that afternoon, four to eight, Tuesdays and Thursdays and every other Saturday, he sold a man from Springfield a serpentine belt and told him, correctly, that the tensioner pulley was the actual problem and the belt alone wouldn’t fix it, and the man looked at him the particular way adults looked at him sometimes, surprised that someone seventeen could be right about something mechanical, and Eli didn’t say anything about that look because there wasn’t anything worth saying. He just wrote up the second part.

The Punnett square thing was worth a minute of his attention on the walk to the bus, because it had done something to him he hadn’t expected.

He’d been failing at it for a week. Not failing the class; failing the concept, which was worse, because the class he could pass by pattern-matching and he knew he could and it made him feel like a fraud in a way he had no interest in examining.

The specific problem had been that he could produce the right answer without knowing why, and he had been able to do that with a great many things for a great many years, and it had gotten him through three schools and four houses, and he had a private rule about it which was: anything you can only do by pattern is a thing that will fail you the first time the pattern changes.

He’d sat at the kitchen table two weeks ago getting quietly furious about it, and Deb had made him put the pencil down and eat something.

That was all she’d done. She hadn’t explained it. She could not have explained it; she’d have been the first to say so. She’d said “eat something” and put a plate down and gone back to what she was doing, and forty minutes later he’d come back to the diagrams with a full stomach and it had opened like a door.

He got a ninety-four.

And what he’d thought, sitting in Ms. Alvarez’s room with the paper in his hand, was not I’m good at this. It was: there is a house I can be stupid in for forty minutes without it costing me anything.

He had never had one of those. That was the actual finding, and it was worth more than the ninety-four, and there was no possible way to say it to anybody at all.

* * *

His manager, Gary, fifty-something and built like a filing cabinet, came by around six with two coffees, one of which he set down in front of Eli without a word, which was as close as Gary came to saying anything kind out loud, and said, “Heard you’re graduating in June.”

“Yeah.”

“Good. You want more hours after, come talk to me. I mean that.”

“I know you do.”

Gary looked at him a second longer than the sentence needed, like he was deciding whether to say something else, and then didn’t, which Eli appreciated more than he’d have known how to explain. Some people, when they found out what you were, tried to say something about it. Gary just handed you a coffee and offered you hours. Eli had decided a while back that the second kind of person was worth more than the first, by a wide margin.

* * *

The afternoon at that school ran on a schedule so fixed that Deb could have told you, at any given moment between six-thirty and two, what she would be doing and where her hands would be.

Eleven-fifteen: sixth grade. Eleven-forty-five: seventh. Twelve-fifteen: eighth, who ate like men and complained like lawyers. Twelve-fifty: the line closed and the second half of the job started, which was the half nobody saw.

Wipe-down. Dish pit. Counts. Temperature logs, which were a legal document and which she had never once falsified and had been asked to twice, both times in 2013, by a district employee who no longer worked there. Waste log. Tomorrow’s thaw pulled from the freezer to the walk-in, which had to happen by one-thirty or the thing would not be thawed by six-thirty and the whole of tomorrow would be an improvisation.

Then the paperwork, which had grown every single year since 1999 and which nobody in the district office had ever once acknowledged was growing.

At one-forty Andre the dishwasher put his head around the office door and said, “The disposal’s making the noise.”

“Which noise?”

“The bad one.”

“Is it the bad one or is it the other bad one?”

“It’s the one from October,” Andre said.

Deb put her pen down. “That’s the other bad one. That’s a spoon.”

It was a spoon. It was always a spoon. She had been retrieving spoons from that disposal since the Clinton administration, and she got it out in ninety seconds with the water off and her sleeve rolled, and Andre said “how do you always know” and Deb said “because in twenty years there has never once been anything in there except a spoon,” and went back to the temperature logs.

* * *

Deb got home at five forty, later than usual because the walk-in cooler at school had thrown a fit and she’d had to wait for the repair guy, and found the house smelling faintly of somebody else’s dinner, which meant Eli had gotten home before her and eaten something and cleaned up after himself well enough that she couldn’t tell what, exactly, which was its own small miracle for a seventeen-year-old.

He was at the kitchen table with the biology test in front of him, not looking at it exactly, just having it there the way you keep a good grade around for a minute before you put it away.

“Ninety-four,” he said, without her asking.

“Look at you.”

“Punnett squares.” Like that explained something, and to her it did, a little, because she’d sat with him at this same table two weeks ago while he’d tried to get the diagrams to make sense and gotten quietly furious with himself in the specific, contained way he got furious, no yelling, just his jaw working and his pencil pressing too hard into the paper, and she’d made him put the pencil down and eat something and come back to it, and apparently that had worked, or something had.

“I’m making the chicken thing,” she said. “The one with the lemon.”

“You always make too much.”

“I like leftovers.”

He looked at her for a second in the particular way he had, assessing, like he was deciding whether to let something go or not, and then let it go, because they both knew she cooked for four in a house of two and had for three and a half years, and neither of them had ever said out loud what that was actually about.

She made the chicken. He did his English reading at the table while she cooked, and somewhere in there Marisol texted her a picture of her own dinner disaster, something involving a casserole and a smoke detector, and Deb laughed out loud in her kitchen for no reason Eli could see, and he looked up.

“Marisol set something on fire again.”

“Just a little.”

“She’s set something on fire like six times this year.”

“Seven, if you count the Crock-Pot in February.”

He almost smiled. Deb counted that as a win, the almost, the way you’d count a coin you found on the sidewalk, small, unearned, worth bending down for anyway.

They ate at six-thirty, the television off, the window over the sink gone fully dark by then, April not yet warm enough to argue with January’s habits, and it was, on the surface, an entirely ordinary Tuesday, a woman and a boy who was not legally hers eating chicken with lemon in a kitchen that smelled like garlic and dish soap, and neither of them mentioned the calendar on the fridge, the one with April third boxed in red, eighty-seven days out, eighty-six by morning, closing in the same unhurried way it had been closing since before Christmas.

Deb washed the dishes. Eli dried, because that was the arrangement, had been since the first month, back when he still flinched slightly if she reached past him too fast and she’d learned to always tell him where her hands were going before they went there. He didn’t flinch anymore. She still told him anyway. Some habits you kept not because they were needed but because of what it meant that you’d started them.

“I got the schedule for the Bridges thing,” she said, drying her hands on the dish towel, casual, the way you’d mention the weather, though she’d rehearsed saying it exactly like that in the car on the way home. “Renee sent it over. Said if you want to look at it before, then…”

“I saw it.”

“Okay.”

“I’m not doing it, Deb.”

“I know. I’m not saying you have to look at it to do it. I’m saying you can look at it.”

He set the last plate in the rack, careful, the way he did everything with his hands, like the plate had a right to be handled gently regardless of what he currently thought about anything else in his life.

“I already know what I want,” he said, and it wasn’t sharp, wasn’t a door closing, just a fact laid down flat on the counter between them the way he laid down most facts, without heat, without asking her to feel any particular way about it.

“Okay,” she said again, and meant it as far as it went, and did not say the rest of what was sitting behind her ribs, which had no good shape yet, which she wasn’t sure she had a right to say to a boy who owed her, by any honest accounting, absolutely nothing at all.

Outside, the last of the salt-gray snow along the curb had gone soft at the edges, the first real thaw of the year, not that either of them was watching for it.
